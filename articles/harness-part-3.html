Monday Morning

Part III: Stop measuring access. Start measuring whether the work got better.

Field note / Gus Halwani

We have the idea. We have the stress test. Now comes the dangerous part: making a program out of it.

Please do not start with a giant transformation program.

Access is not value

Licenses activated. Weekly prompts. Percentage of staff with access.

Those numbers tell you that something was distributed. They do not tell you whether the work improved.

I care more about value density:

  • Which specific tasks became faster, better, or more consistent?
  • Where did human review catch something important?
  • Where was human review just ceremonial friction?
  • Which workflow learned from last month’s mistakes?
  • Where is risk quietly accumulating because the dashboard cannot see it?

If you cannot answer those questions, you do not have an AI performance system yet. You have a tooling footprint.

Start with three tasks

Not three departments. Three tasks.

Pick work that happens often enough to learn from, matters enough to justify attention, and is bounded enough that you can tell what changed.

For each one, write down:

  1. What better means. Speed? Quality? Fewer missed signals? Less variance?
  2. How bad bad can be. Annoying rework and a clinical decision are not the same failure.
  3. Where the human belongs. Initiate, review, approve, audit, or stay out of the way.
  4. What the model can touch. Tools, data, permissions, and hard boundaries.
  5. What you will learn this week. One hypothesis. One observable result.

Build the smallest honest loop

Give the pilot enough reality to teach you something, but keep rollback easy.

Instrument what matters. Save examples. Look at the misses, not only the attractive outputs somebody remembered to screenshot. Change one part of the harness at a time when you can.

Weekly is usually a better rhythm than quarterly. The system is moving too fast for ceremonial learning.

Look for harness builders

The scarce person is not necessarily the one who knows the most model names.

It is the person who can connect model behavior to workflow design, human judgment, controls, and evidence. Someone who can see the machine and the room around the machine.

That blend is still rare. It is also where the leverage is.

The split that matters

The next divide will not be between organizations that use AI and organizations that do not. Almost everybody will use it.

The divide will be between organizations that bought capability and organizations that built a way to learn.

One has tools.

The other compounds.

That is the strategy.
