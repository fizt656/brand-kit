Agents Are the New Attack Surface

The interface feels like a chat window. The runtime may be holding your keys.

Field note / Gus Halwani

The easy version of this story is panic.

I do not think panic is useful.

I do think we are kidding ourselves when we talk about agent risk as if it lives entirely inside “the model.”

The model is one part. The real behavior comes from the rig around it: files, tools, browser access, shell access, memory, credentials, permissions, and whatever instructions wandered in through the side door.

The rig matters

A great guitarist through a broken cable, noisy pedal chain, and bad amp is going to have a night.

Agents are like that, except the pedalboard may have access to your email.

This is the security version of the same point I made in The Harness Is the Strategy: capability does not become behavior until it enters a system.

A serious organization can isolate runtimes, use low-privilege accounts, separate sensitive data, require approvals, and watch what the system is doing. None of that is exotic. It is just engineering.

But the interesting—and slightly alarming—change is who is experimenting now.

It looks simpler than it is

Cloning a repo and trying a local agent used to be fairly niche behavior. Now curious professionals, hobbyists, and small teams are doing it because the interface has become wonderfully approachable.

That is good. It is also where the mismatch appears.

It feels like trying a new app.

It may actually be closer to giving a very eager intern your badge, your browser, a ring of keys, and a vague instruction.

The intern is fast, literal in surprising places, and occasionally reads hostile text from strangers.

AI literacy has to grow up

AI literacy cannot stop at “how do I prompt this?”

People need a few deeper reflexes:

  • What can this system touch?
  • What secrets are within reach?
  • Which outside inputs can influence it?
  • What actions require approval?
  • How do I stop it, inspect it, and recover?

You do not need everyone to become a security engineer. You do need people to recognize when they are operating a runtime instead of chatting with a webpage.

A few boring habits

Isolate what matters. Minimize permissions. Keep secrets out of easy reach. Treat outside text as potentially hostile. Use disposable environments when you are testing strange things. Make consequential actions ask first.

Boring habits are underrated. They are what let the interesting experiments continue.

That is the tension I keep geeking out on: how much technical understanding does an ordinary smart person need to experiment near the edge without becoming reckless?

Because agents are spreading faster than the habits required to use them well.

The answer is not to close the door.

It is to learn where the hinges are.
