The Signal Thread

Why I Think in First Principles

Maps are useful. Until the territory changes.

Field note / Gus Halwani

My career makes more sense backwards.

Sound engineering. Neuroscience. Teaching. AI. On paper, it looks like I kept changing subjects. I did not. I kept following the same thing: signals, what they mean, and what happens when they reach another person.

That is probably why I keep returning to first principles. When the labels change—and in AI they change every twelve minutes—the underlying questions are more reliable.

The thread is the useful part

In sound, the source is not the sound you hear. The room matters. The microphone matters. The cable, the preamp, the person at the console, and the expectations of the listener all matter.

Brains are not much different. Neither are classrooms. Neither are AI systems.

A model does not create value in isolation. A learner does not learn because information was technically delivered. A mix does not work because every track is audible. The whole system has to resolve into something meaningful for a human being.

Once you see that pattern, the career stops looking random.

Compasses, not maps

A map tells you where somebody else found solid ground. A compass helps when the ground has moved.

Right now, a lot of AI advice is map-shaped: use this model, copy this prompt, adopt this framework, repeat this maturity curve. Some of it is useful. None of it survives contact with every organization, every task, or every person.

So I start lower down:

  • What are we actually trying to improve?
  • What would count as evidence that it improved?
  • Where can this fail, and who absorbs the failure?
  • What does the human do better here?
  • What can the machine do that changes the shape of the work?

Those questions are not glamorous. Good. Glamour is usually where the signal gets noisy.

The human part is not decoration

My father could talk to anyone. He changed his cadence without making a performance of it. He met people where they were, and they felt it.

I think about that constantly in education and AI. Understanding is not just accuracy. It is contact. The best system in the world still has to reach a real person, in a real context, with all the ambiguity that comes with being alive.

That does not make rigor less important. It makes rigor harder.

Build the learning loop

I do not think organizations need a perfect map for AI. There is no perfect map. I think they need a way to learn without lying to themselves.

Start with a real task. Make the outcome observable. Put people in the loop on purpose, not because a policy slide said so. Watch what fails. Change the system. Repeat.

That is the first-principles habit I trust: keep the question stable long enough to notice when the answer changes.

The tools will keep moving. Fine. The compass still works.
