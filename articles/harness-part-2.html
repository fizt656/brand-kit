Does the Harness Hold?

Part II: Three tests for the moment reality stops behaving.

Field note / Gus Halwani

Part I made the argument: the model is not the strategy. The harness around it is.

Fine. Now how do we know if the harness is any good?

I keep coming back to three tests. They borrow from Nassim Taleb, but this is not a book report. I am using the ideas as a wrench.

1. What happens when the weird thing happens?

Every AI demo lives in the happy path. Production does not.

The useful question is not whether you predicted every failure. You did not. It is whether the system can tell a beneficial surprise from a dangerous one.

Maybe the model finds a connection nobody expected. Great. Maybe it acts on an instruction hidden in a document. Less great.

The harness needs different terrain for different stakes: freedom to explore where mistakes are cheap, tighter boundaries where they are not, and an escalation path that does not require a pilgrimage through governance.

When something strange happens, does the system learn and route it? Or does everyone panic and ban the entire category?

2. Does failure make it smarter?

A robust system survives stress. A learning system uses stress.

Models change. Prompts drift. Data shifts. People invent uses nobody approved because nobody imagined them. This is not an edge case. This is the operating environment.

After an incident, I want to know:

  • Did we capture enough context to understand what happened?
  • Did the repair become part of the system, or stay in someone’s memory?
  • Did we change the right boundary, or just add friction everywhere?
  • Did human attention move toward the places where it is actually valuable?

If every mistake produces a blanket restriction, the harness may be getting “safer” while the organization gets more fragile.

3. Who feels the consequence?

This is the least technical test and usually the one that matters most.

One team builds. Another team operates. A third team reports the success metric. The person doing the cleanup has no authority over the design.

That arrangement can make almost anything look good for a quarter.

Authority and consequence need to touch. The people closest to failure should influence the system. Metrics should include rework, escalation burden, and error cost—not just licenses, prompts, and hours allegedly saved.

If accountability floats upward while consequences flow downward, the harness will look excellent on slides and crack exactly where people live.

The point

A good AI system does not eliminate uncertainty. It gives uncertainty somewhere intelligent to go.

It preserves useful surprise, gets better from pressure, and makes the people with authority feel enough of the outcome to care.

That is a much harder standard than “the demo worked.” It is also the only one that matters once the demo ends.
